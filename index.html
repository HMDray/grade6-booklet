Section 02 — Reading & Writing

Key Skills for the New Grade

📖 Reading Literature
SkillWhat It Means
Theme developmentYou don't just identify the theme: you trace how the author builds it across the whole story.
Dialogue & characterThe way a character speaks tells you a lot about who they are. What does their language reveal?
Point of viewWho is telling the story, and how does that change what the reader knows? A first-person narrator can only tell you what THEY see.
Word choice & toneAuthors choose every word deliberately. Why THAT word, instead of a simpler one?
Comparing two storiesWhen two stories share a similar theme, what's the same? What's different? Why does it matter?
📰 Reading Informational Texts
SkillWhat It Means
Tracing an argumentFollow the author's reasoning: What is their claim? What evidence do they use? Is it convincing?
Evaluating sourcesNot all sources are trustworthy! Ask: Who wrote it? When? Do they have expertise?
Counter-argumentsStrong writers bring up the other side and explain why their view is stronger.
Text structureHOW a text is organised (cause/effect, compare/contrast) affects how convincing it is.
Comparing two authorsTwo writers can cover the same topic but present it very differently. Can you spot why?
✏️ Writing
SkillWhat It Means
Counter-argumentA strong argument addresses the OTHER side first, then explains why your view is stronger.
Types of evidenceMix your evidence: use quotes, statistics, anecdotes, and examples.
Specific thesisYour thesis must be ARGUABLE. 'Friendship is important' is NOT a thesis. 'The author uses conflict to show loyalty is more valuable than popularity' IS a thesis.
TransitionsParagraphs shouldn't just stop: each one should lead smoothly into the next.
Revising for strengthAsk yourself: Is every paragraph connected to my thesis? Is my evidence the BEST I could use?

Speaking, Listening & Language

🗣️ Speaking & Listening
SkillWhat It Means
Using evidenceDon't just share your opinion — say WHERE in the text you found your evidence.
Building on ideasInstead of starting a new topic, respond to what someone just said: 'I'd like to add to that…'
Challenging politelyYou CAN disagree! Try: 'That's interesting, but have you considered…'
Formal vs. informalTalking to a friend is different from presenting to your class. Practise switching between the two.
🤔 Language
SkillWhat It Means
Connotation vs. denotation'Home' and 'house' mean the same thing, but feel very different. Every word has a literal meaning AND an emotional meaning.
Greek & Latin roots'Bio' means life. 'Graph' means write. Learning roots helps you decode new words instantly.
Precise vocabularyUpgrade vague words. Instead of 'said' try: insisted, whispered, argued, declared, snapped.
Active vs. passive voiceActive is more powerful: 'The author reveals the theme' beats 'The theme is revealed by the author.'

Section 03 — Essay Writing
Writing Vocabulary: Words to Know
🎣
HOOK
Grab the reader's attention: a question, a surprising fact, or a bold statement. Never begin with 'In this essay I will…'!
📋
CONTEXT
Give your reader background: tell them the title, author, and what the text is about.
🎯
THESIS
Your MAIN CLAIM: one specific, arguable sentence at the end of your introduction. The 'big promise' of your essay.
☕ The TEA Paragraph Structure
🗨️
T — TOPIC SENTENCE
Start every body paragraph with a clear argument. It must connect directly to your thesis.
📌
E — EVIDENCE
Include a quote or specific example. Introduce it first: never drop a quote in without context.
💡
A — ANALYSIS
Explain WHY your evidence matters. What does the word choice / image / event actually reveal?
✅
CONCLUSION
Restate your thesis in NEW words. Summarise your key arguments. End with a final thought.

Section 04 — MLA Format

Citing Your Sources

MLA format is how academic writers present their work. Your teacher will expect it — so let's make it simple! 📐

📄 Your MLA Heading — top-left of your first page

What to WriteExample
Your first and last nameAlex Johnson
Your teacher's nameMs. Rodriguez
Your class name and periodEnglish 6
The date (day month year)15 June 2025

🔤 In-Text Citations

At the end of a sentence with a quote, write the author's last name and page number in brackets before the full stop.

📖 Novel or Article:The author reveals that "true friendship means being honest, even when it hurts" (Smith 42).
📜 Poem (use line numbers):"Do not go gentle into that good night" (Thomas 1).

📚 Works Cited — Format Examples

Book:Jones, Maria. The Digital Age. Penguin, 2019.
Article:Ahmed, Sam. "Screen Time and Learning." Education Weekly, vol. 5, 2022, pp. 20–23.
Website:Lee, Tom. "Digital Limits." TeenLearning.org, 5 Jan. 2024, www.teenlearning.org/limits.

✅ Five Rules to Remember

  • Your essay title: centred at the top; not bold, not in quotes, not underlined.
  • Titles of full works (novels, films): italicised or for handwritten, "quotation marks" — e.g., The Outsidersor "The Outsiders".
  • Titles of short works (poems, articles): in "quotation marks".
  • Double-space everything; no extra spaces between paragraphs.
  • Works Cited: hanging indent (second line indented 0.5 inches, first line flush).

Section 05 — Word Power

Words to Know

These are the key words your teacher will expect you to know AND USE. Don't just memorise them — try to use them in your writing this summer! ✨

WordWhat It Means
themeThe BIG message or lesson of a story: what it's really about beneath the plot
connotationThe emotional feeling of a word, beyond its literal dictionary meaning
denotationThe literal, dictionary definition of a word: its 'official' meaning
dictionThe specific words an author chooses: and why they chose them over other options
point of viewWho is narrating the story and what that narrator can and cannot know
counter-argumentThe opposing view that a writer brings up: and then argues against
rebuttalYour response to a counter-argument: the reason YOUR view is stronger
inferenceA smart conclusion drawn from clues in the text: like a detective's deduction
nuanceThe subtle shades of meaning that make the difference between almost-right and exactly-right
syntaxThe way sentences are constructed: short and punchy vs. long and flowing

💬 What's the difference between 'said' and 'whispered'? That difference is CONNOTATION!

⭐ Pick a favourite character. What THREE words best describe them, and why those specific words?

Section 06 — Grammar Essentials

Grammar for Grade 6 (CCSS L.6.1)

In Grade 6, grammar focuses on pronouns : knowing which form to use and making sure they are clear, consistent, and correctly placed. 💪
👤 Pronoun Case — Subjective, Objective & Possessive
CaseWhen to Use ItExamples
Subjective
(doing the action)		Use when the pronoun is the subject of a verb.She read the book. He and I went together.
Objective
(receiving the action)		Use when the pronoun is the object, or follows a preposition.The teacher praised him. Between you and me…
Possessive
(shows ownership)		Shows something belongs to someone. No apostrophe needed!Their book. Its cover. Our class project.
💥 Intensive Pronouns — Adding Emphasis
PronounsWhat They DoExample
myself / yourself
himself / herself
ourselves / themselves		Adds emphasis. The sentence still makes sense without it — that's how you know it's intensive!The author herself attended. I myself was surprised.
⚠️ Watch out!Myself is NOT a polite substitute for I or me.Give it to myself. → Give it to me.
🔍 Vague Pronoun Reference — Be Specific!
ProblemWhy It's VagueFix It
Unclear antecedentThe reader can't tell which noun the pronoun refers to.Maya told Sofia that she won. → Maya told Sofia that Maya had won.
Broad reference'This' or 'which' refers to a whole idea, not a specific noun.She forgot her homework, which was embarrassing. → Forgetting her homework embarrassed her.
⚠️
Common Pronoun Errors to Fix

Me and Jake presented first. → Jake and I presented first. (subjective case)

Their going to the library. → They're going to the library. (their ≠ they're)

Section 07 — Punctuation Guide

Punctuation for Grade 6 (CCSS L.6.2)

Grade 6 punctuation focuses on nonrestrictive elements : extra information added to a sentence using commas, parentheses, or dashes. 🚦
, Commas — Setting Off Extra Information
RuleWhyExample
Nonrestrictive clauseExtra info that could be removed without changing the meaning: set it off with two commas.My sister, who lives in Paris, visits every summer.
Appositive phraseA phrase that renames the noun next to it.Our teacher, Ms. Ramos, assigned three chapters.
Introductory elementAfter an introductory word, phrase, or clause at the start of a sentence.After finishing the novel, we discussed the ending.
InterrupterA word or phrase that interrupts the flow: set it off with two commas.The poem, in my opinion, has a hopeful tone.
( ) Parentheses — Adding Extra Information
UseRuleExample
Additional detailAdd a useful fact or explanation that is not essential to the sentence.The poem (written in 1920) has three stanzas.
AbbreviationDefine an abbreviation the first time you use it.Common Core State Standards (CCSS) guide the curriculum.
Punctuation tipIf the parenthetical is inside a sentence, the period goes OUTSIDE the closing bracket.She chose the harder option (which surprised everyone).
— Dashes — Emphasis and Drama
UseRuleExample
Single dashIntroduces a summary, explanation, or dramatic pause at the end of a clause.He only wanted one thing — to be free.
Double dashesEnclose a parenthetical element more dramatically than commas, great for emphasis.The decision — made in seconds — changed everything.

Am I Ready?

Go through this list honestly. Tick only the skills you are genuinely confident in. Leave the rest — those are your summer goals! 🎯
  • I can explain how an author builds a theme across a whole story, not just say what the theme is
  • I can find specific evidence in a text and explain clearly why it supports my argument
  • I can analyse how point of view affects what the reader knows and feels
  • I can identify the connotation of a word and explain its effect on the reader
  • I can write a TEA paragraph: Topic Sentence → Evidence → Analysis
  • I can write an argumentative essay that includes a counter-argument paragraph
  • I can write a specific, arguable thesis statement : not just a topic
  • I can trace and evaluate the argument in a nonfiction text
  • I can write a Works Cited entry for a book and for a website
  • I know the five essential MLA formatting rules
  • I can use pronouns in the correct case (subjective, objective, possessive)
  • I can use intensive pronouns correctly (myself, herself, themselves, etc.)
  • I can identify and fix a vague pronoun reference in my writing
  • I can use commas to set off nonrestrictive clauses and appositives
  • I can use dashes and parentheses correctly to add parenthetical information

Section 09 — Let's Practise!

Summer Activities

Try at least ONE of these activities before school starts. Write your answers right in the booklet! ✏️

📖
Point of View SwapCreative Challenge
⭐

Take a scene from a story you know well. Rewrite it from a completely different character's perspective. Then write 2–3 sentences: How does the change in narrator change what the reader knows and feels?

📰
Argument DetectiveCritical Thinking Challenge
⭐⭐

Find a short opinion article on a topic you care about. Identify: (1) the author's main claim, (2) two pieces of evidence they use, (3) any counter-arguments they bring up. Then write one paragraph: Is their argument convincing? Why or why not?

💬
Connotation ExplorerWord Power Challenge
⭐⭐

Find five pairs of words with the same denotation but different connotations (e.g., thin / skinny, confident / arrogant). For each pair, explain the difference in feeling. Then use three of them in sentences about a character from a book.

🔍
Pronoun PatrolGrammar Challenge
⭐⭐⭐

Find a paragraph in any book or article. Underline every pronoun. Identify its case (subjective, objective, or possessive) and check that the antecedent (the noun it refers to) is clear. Then rewrite one sentence using an intensive pronoun correctly.
